The platform in practice

Building the OneSphere pilot on finOS

A real lender, a real 43-page requirements document, and a concrete plan for standing up a licensed digital-lending pilot in Malaysia — on a platform Swift builds and owns.

The programme

OneSphere intends to serve migrant workers in Malaysia — a segment traditional credit files describe poorly, but whose remittance behaviour is a genuine cash-flow signal.

Launch product: Remit Now Pay Later (RNPL), credit extended to a sender at remit-time and repaid later; a short-tenure salary-linked loan follows later. Corridor lending is the same mechanic wherever it runs — only the corridor, the partner and the regulatory pack change. OneSphere has not lent yet, so there is no loan book to migrate, and it has no accounting system — finOS native accounting goes live with the first release.

What each layer supplies

finOS is a self-contained lending platform: the lending domain, the platform services it runs on, and the channels above them. The pilot-specific build concentrates on credit truth and the Malaysian regulatory pack.

The platform is already there
Identity, documents, notifications, task management, integration, model serving, and the runtime and CI/CD foundation are part of finOS. The pilot configures them; it does not build them.
finOS supplies credit truth
The ledger, credit operations, decisioning policy, servicing and collections. This is the part where correctness cannot be compromised.
OneSphere is configuration
The pilot is client-specific configuration and a regulatory pack over the shared lending domain, with no product logic written for one client.
Language is configuration
A borrower journey in Nepali, Bengali or Bahasa Malaysia is translation content over the same server-driven journey — not a separate build.

The approval sequence shapes the build

Verified against the regulator's own FAQ, checklist and guidelines: online moneylending approval is an endorsement on an existing licence, and what the regulator asks for is a documentation pack plus a system security certificate — not a live demonstration. A search of all three official documents found no published demo, inspection or system audit as an approval step.

  1. 01 · DONE

    Conventional licence issued

    OneSphere holds its KPKT moneylending licence. More than 90 days of remaining validity is required when the endorsement is applied for.

  2. 02

    Documented design + security certificate

    A practical plan of the site, application and customer journey, including the technology used.

  3. 03

    Conditional approval

    Assessed by an internal committee. Service standard is three to six months from a complete application being tabled.

  4. 04

    Build, register domain, full approval

    The public register names the specific URL or app tied to each approval, so an identifiable platform must exist.

The plan is the application

The first deliverable is a document describing the system and the technology behind it. That document is far stronger when the platform it describes already carries identity, encryption, secrets management, audit trails and observability as platform properties, rather than as pilot-specific work.

The customer-journey and technology plan is therefore a first-class deliverable of the engagement — it is, quite literally, the application.

Where the approval stands

OneSphere's KPKT moneylending licence is issued, so the online endorsement can now be applied for — with the finOS system plan as the application. The regulator's service standard is three to six months from a complete application, and backlog is a documented risk.

Two questions still shape the product: the regulator's advice that online loans go to citizens and permanent residents, and the attestation every loan agreement requires. That is an argument for a smaller, compliance-first first release.

Four phases, smallest risky thing first

PHASE 0

Fit spike

Prove the ledger pattern under pilot volumes, prove the decisioning path under real connector fan-out, and confirm which shared services are production-grade for this workload. Stood up as a single-node topology. The point is to convert the largest architectural assumption into a measured result before anything is frozen.

PHASE 1

Licence-readiness release

The compliance-critical spine, plus the documentation the application requires: onboarding with KYC and consent, agreement generation with the attestation step designed in, the credit kernel and servicing ledger under the Malaysian guardrail pack, native accounting (general ledger, cash and bank, financial statements, since OneSphere has no accounting system of its own), the audit trio, and access control. Commercially rich modules are deliberately deferred. Deliverables include the customer-journey and technology plan, the security certificate evidence, and a registered .my domain with a named application.

PHASE 2

Pilot operation

Remit Now Pay Later live on one corridor. Remittance regularity as the credit signal, scored on the finOS model-serving stack with reason codes and model versioning. Repayment by direct debit through the payments module, collections with contact caps and quiet hours enforced by the platform, and portfolio reporting from the operational record.

PHASE 3

Second corridor

A new corridor as new configuration — a regulatory pack over the same lending domain, not another build. This is the phase that turns a project into a product, and it is the reason the first build is worth doing on a platform at all.

Two things this deployment must get right

Two decisions that lending, specifically, cannot get wrong.

  1. 01

    The kernel keeps one transactional boundary

    Balances, credit operations, schedules and allocation commit in a single ACID transaction. Saga appears only at external edges — payment rails, the remittance leg, core-banking posting.

  2. 02

    Statutory limits sit outside the scripts

    The rate ceiling, the compounding prohibition and the fee prohibition belong in the guardrail layer, where a pricing-script edit cannot breach them. Under this statute a mistake is not a pricing error — sums paid are set off against the principal.

What primary-source research changed

Verified against the statute and the regulator's own published documents — findings the requirements document did not contain, each one changing the product before it was built.

Finding 01

The target market is at risk

Published guidance advises that online loans be extended only to citizens and permanent residents — directly adverse to a migrant-worker product. Advisory rather than statutory, but approval runs through a discretionary committee. This is the first question to put in writing.

Finding 02

There is no separate online licence

It is an endorsement on an existing moneylending licence, applied for physically, with no additional fee and no separate validity. Programme terminology that treats it as a distinct licence class misstates both the sequence and the eligibility test.

Finding 03

Agreements need human attestation

The statute requires a qualified person to explain the terms and certify the borrower appears to understand them. An unattested agreement is void — the hardest constraint on a fully remote journey, and the requirements document does not address it anywhere.

And one that decides a product

The governing statute caps unsecured interest, prohibits compounding, and makes any additional fee illegal — with sums paid set off against principal. On a one-month tenure that leaves roughly 1.5% of principal as total revenue, with no fee available to bridge it.

The short-tenure salary-linked product is very unlikely to be viable as specified. The remittance-linked product, at larger tickets and longer tenures, is materially stronger. That is a commercial finding worth more to a lender than the software that would have implemented it.

If an Islamic variant is wanted, decide it before design

Remit Now Pay Later is genuinely hard to structure as Shariah-compliant, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than assuming it will work. The product hands a customer cash to remit, and cash cannot be lent at a profit. A commodity-trade structure works and is accepted in the market, but it is operationally heavy for a small, fast, low-margin financing. An interest-free loan paired with a genuine agency fee for the remittance service is the cleanest and least system-heavy route — but only if the fee reflects real service cost rather than profit on the loan in disguise, and that is a question only a Shariah committee can settle.

The requirements document also treats late payment as ordinary revenue, which no compliant structure permits. Both points belong in a committee conversation that starts before product design, not after.

Since then, primary-source research settled three more — and each one reached the guardrails

Finding 04 · Margin

Reconciled to the enacted directive

Nepal's Margin Trading Directive 2082 (in force February 2026) superseded the secondary figures the plan carried — broker capacity is 5× net worth, not 2×; a single client is capped at 10% of the total facility, not 20% of net worth — and added a 4.5× funding-leverage limit and a 20% maintenance margin. Each reached the guardrails as a rule, not a note.

Finding 05 · Corridors

A remittance licence never lends

Across five markets a remittance or exchange licence confers no lending permission on its own — a separately-licensed lender must originate. One corridor partner already runs exactly this, lending to foreign workers through its own licensed finance entity over the Gulf wage-protection rail. So the platform powers the licensed lender; the remittance operator distributes.

Finding 06 · Islamic

Sharia products don't cross borders

A structure accepted in one market is not automatically accepted in another — commodity-Tawarruq is the backbone of Malaysian personal finance yet is disfavoured under the Gulf's AAOIFI standard. A product cannot simply be ported; each market gets its own Shariah pack, versioned and effective-dated like every other regulatory pack.

Open before scope freeze

Stated plainly, because each one changes the build and none of them is ours to answer alone.

  • Will the regulator approve online lending to documented work-permit holders?Nothing else matters if the answer is no. It should be put in writing first.
  • Does the conditional-approval tier still operate in 2026?It determines whether the platform is built before or after the application — the single largest planning variable.
  • What satisfies the system security certificate?A digital certificate from a licensed authority and a security assurance report are different procurements.
  • How is attestation performed in an online journey?Whatever mechanism is permitted shapes the entire customer flow, so it must be settled before UX design.

One caveat recorded honestly: the regulator's 27-page guidelines document is an image-only scan and has been read only in part. Its full conduct, security, record-keeping and disclosure requirements remain the most important outstanding input to this plan.

Where it runs first

OneSphere Malaysia first, then four of Swift's own platforms

The kernel is market-agnostic and product-agnostic by construction. After OneSphere, Swift's own four platforms are the next deployments and the hardest test — four genuinely different lending businesses, one kernel, no fork.

A bank, a licensed lender, a wallet operator or a broker gets the same kernel the estates run on — in their own environment or on finOS SaaS, with their own regulatory pack, their own products and their own data. Nothing about the four estates below is privileged: they are simply the deployments Swift runs itself, and the reason the abstractions are honest rather than theoretical.

Why wallet first among Swift's platforms

Scored against integration complexity, regulatory load, product complexity, counterparty readiness, revenue and learning value, the wallet vertical wins on every axis except revenue — and revenue is precisely the axis a first vertical should not maximise. The first vertical's job is to prove the kernel with the smallest failure blast radius.

It also exercises the full loan lifecycle at high volume within months, so every kernel path is tested early rather than discovered late.

Home jurisdiction

Nepal first — the central bank's directives, the credit information bureau, NPA classification and provisioning, and the capital-market regulator for margin. The core stays market-agnostic; Nepal is simply the first regulatory pack, and the UK and Korea enter as corridor considerations rather than as separate builds.

Nepali and Devanagari at launch, the Bikram Sambat calendar rendered alongside Gregorian, and amounts grouped in lakh and crore. A corridor language is a message-catalog addition, not a code change.

The rollout sequence

OneSphere Malaysia is the first deployment on the kernel — a KPKT-licensed lender in a real market. The rest is the order Swift builds its own estates in, each vertical reusing what the previous one proved and nothing sequenced by revenue. An institution deploying the kernel starts wherever its own book starts; the sequence is evidence that the kernel generalises, not a path anyone else has to follow.

  1. PHASE 0

    Kernel spine

    Credit kernel, decisioning, policy registry and guardrails. Nothing customer-facing.

  2. PHASE 0 · FIRST

    OneSphere Malaysia

    First deployment — Remit Now Pay Later for the migrant corridor, KPKT-licensed; the first build of the kernel and the corridor design.

  3. PHASE 1

    Wallet micro-loans and BNPL

    One regulator, one currency, the simplest products and the cleanest seam.

  4. PHASE 2

    SNPL corridors

    Reuses the kernel; adds FX, corridor packs and the policy-safety machinery.

  5. PHASE 3

    Bank retail and corporate

    Adds the core-banking connector, collateral, covenants, maker-checker and provisioning depth.

  6. PHASE 3–4

    Margin

    Bridge over existing loan events first. Absorb the estate's own loan service only on evidence.

Two honest notes. No architecture artifact exists for the digital-banking estate, so that integration is discovery work rather than a documented seam. And the margin platform already runs a complete loan management service — there is no revenue gap there, which is exactly why it is sequenced last and bridged before it is ever absorbed.
