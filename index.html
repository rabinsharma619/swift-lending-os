The operational problem

Most lending stacks make change expensive and truth negotiable

These are not edge cases. They are the predictable result of assembling a lending business from systems that were never designed to share a ledger.

  1. 01

    Every product change is an engineering release

    A new segment, a fee change, a revised cut-off — each becomes a ticket, a sprint and a deployment window. The lead time is long enough that the business stops asking, and the product stops evolving. That is the real cost, and it never appears on an invoice.

  2. 02

    The loan in one system is not the loan in another

    Origination from one vendor, servicing from a second, collections from a third. Balances are reconciled between them on a schedule rather than held once — so reconciliation becomes a permanent staffed function, and disputes are settled by whichever system the operator happens to trust.

  3. 03

    Each new market forks the codebase

    Rate caps, prescribed documents, bureau formats and conduct rules get written into the core for the first market. The second market copies the core and edits it. From then on every fix lands two, three or five times — and eventually stops landing everywhere.

  4. 04

    "Why was this declined in March?" starts a project

    The scorecard has moved on, the bureau response was never kept, and the rule that fired was edited in place. Reconstructing a past decision means archaeology — precisely when a regulator, an ombudsman or a customer is waiting for the answer.

The common root

In each case the thing that must never break and the thing that must change weekly are tangled together. Swift Lending OS separates them: a credit kernel that owns correctness, and a configuration layer that owns speed.

The Building Platform

Prebuilt modules over one credit kernel

The kernel owns what must be correct. The modules and configuration own what must be fast to change.

The credit kernel

One ledger. One transactional boundary.

Every balance change is a discrete, timestamped, reversible, audited operation. No service anywhere writes a balance directly — and any loan's position at any past instant reconstructs from its operation history alone.

  • —Credit operations: accrual, charge, payment, past-due
  • —Balances and payment schedules
  • —Allocation across obligations
  • —Contract types: conventional and sale-based

Multi-channel intake, duplicate detection, KYC/AML and fraud orchestration, credit assessment, profile building, offer generation, document collection and generation, e-signature, approval authority and disbursement. Most applications flow through untouched; underwriters see only what needs judgment.

Scorecards as first-class objects, decision tables and trees, one sandboxed expression runtime across every scope, and a feature store turning raw provider payloads into named business variables. Dry run, replay against historical cohorts, shadow-run on live traffic and champion/challenger — before any policy takes effect.

A two-tier product and variant model, so segment, channel and promotional variations are configuration rather than duplicated products. Pricing scripts read verified profiles; multiple payment-calculation engines; collateral rules and per-product document requirements. A new segment variant goes live in hours, without engineering.

Accrual, charge, payment and past-due operations run on schedule; delinquency ages automatically; restructures, payment holidays, forbearance, payoff quotes, early settlement and write-off are permissioned, validated actions. Staff act on exceptions, not routine — and every intervention is in the audit trail.

Money is captured at the client level and allocated to loans separately — so one transfer covering three loans is one entry, not three. Smart allocation by configured strategy, directed allocation when instructed, clean rollback that preserves history, and continuous reconciliation. Rails, gateway callbacks and bank files land in one model.

A double-entry posting engine, not a report. Every approved credit operation emits its journal in real time through the outbox — so there is no end-of-day batch that can diverge from the operational view, and each entry is balanced by constraint rather than by reconciliation. Multi-entity for the lender of record, multi-currency with the FX rate captured at posting and never re-derived, posting rules as versioned configuration, and enforced accounting periods. Each deploying bank maps the logical account roles onto its own chart of accounts.

Segmentation that selects the next best action per case. Contact-frequency caps and quiet hours are enforced by the platform, not left to agent discipline. Promise-to-pay, hardship and arrangements, charge-off, recoveries and agency placement. Vulnerability flags and third-party representation suppress standard cadences automatically.

Ratio ceilings, coverage floors, insurance currency and review cadence modelled as a metric, a specification and a baseline anchored at signing. Breach is a first-class event on the loan, in the work queue and in reporting — not a rediscovery at the next manual review cycle.

Operational views on live data, portfolio and vintage dashboards on a modelled reporting replica, regulatory returns generated from the operational record, and provisioning inputs for impairment models. Analyst queries never contend with origination latency.

Beneath every module: documents and communications, the connector layer and public API, access control, and the audit trio — entity history, activity log and exception log.

Three layers, one rule between them

SDK

Engineers define what is possible

Entities, business processes and state machines, events, actions, custom credit operations, connectors. Ordinary framework code in your repository.

Configuration

The business defines how it behaves

Products and variants, pricing, workflows and scorecards, feature mappings, templates, roles and queues — versioned, diffable, promotable, reversible.

Operations

Your teams run it

Applications and manual decisions, loan tracking, transactions, collections queues, covenant monitoring, dashboards and exception triage.

The rule. Every configuration option exists because an SDK primitive enables it. When the business needs something configuration cannot express, engineering adds a primitive — and it appears as a new configuration option rather than a bespoke feature.

The constraint that keeps this safe

No artifact carries business logic. Offer mathematics, statutory guardrails, document requirements and notification rules all evaluate server-side — so a channel cannot drift from policy, and a new channel cannot quietly reimplement a rule slightly differently.

Core-banking agnostic, by rule

A bank's first question is whether this works with the core it already runs. It does, because the platform is built to a rule rather than to a vendor: it never assumes it is the ledger of record, and the mapping to the lender's chart of accounts is configuration rather than code.

One contract, per-core adapters

Modern cores speak REST and JSON, older ones SOAP and XML, and some only exchange files overnight. All three sit behind a single internal contract, so the lending domain is written once and the difference is an adapter — not a variant of the product.

  • —Loan mirroring so the customer sees the loan in the banking channels and statements they already use
  • —Disbursement posting to the customer's deposit account
  • —Repayment intake from standing orders and branch deposits
  • —Summarised GL handoff, with per-loan detail staying in the platform
  • —End-of-day reconciliation with breaks raised as exceptions rather than absorbed silently

The same rule covers the rest of the stack. Payment rails, bureaus, identity providers and messaging all sit behind connector contracts with the same shape — so no deployment is wedged to one vendor's core, one rail, or one bureau. Replacing any of them is an adapter and a configuration change, not a migration.

The load-bearing decision

Saga at the external edges. Never inside the balance.

Eventual consistency between a payment and the balance it settles is precisely how lending platforms develop subtle, expensive, late-discovered defects.

A general platform's default — one database per service with saga between them — is right for most domains and wrong for a loan ledger. The credit vertical is sized to contain the whole ledger; everything genuinely distributed sits outside it with compensating transactions.

Six invariants the architecture carries — and tests

Not aspirations. Each is enforced structurally, and each is testable.

  1. 01

    Balances change only through credit operations

    No service, adapter or batch job writes a balance column. Enforced by architecture tests.

  2. 02

    Money is integer minor units

    Floating point in a money path is a build failure, caught by static analysis.

  3. 03

    Events publish only from the outbox

    In the same transaction as the state change — no phantom events, none lost.

  4. 04

    Decisions are versioned and replayable

    Identical recorded inputs produce identical outputs, years later.

  5. 05

    Guardrails run outside policy scripts

    A script may propose any rate; the guardrail layer clamps or rejects it.

  6. 06

    Every topology runs the same artifacts

    Including air-gapped. No capability works only in a hosted deployment.

    Compliance built into the architecture

    Structural, not bolted on

    Audit trail, decision reproducibility, statutory limits and data residency are properties of how the platform is built — which is why they cannot be switched off by a configuration mistake.

    Guardrails execute outside policy scripts

    A pricing script may propose any rate. The guardrail layer clamps or rejects it — and records that it did. If statutory caps lived inside the scripts business users edit, a well-meaning change could breach a rate ceiling, a tenor limit or a fee prohibition.

    • —Interest and profit-rate ceilings
    • —Permitted fees, and prohibitions
    • —Default-charge limits and basis
    • —Amount caps and tenor limits
    • —Mandated allocation order
    • —Contact caps and quiet hours

    Any decision, replayed exactly, years later

    "Why was this application declined in March?" is a question the architecture answers directly — not one that starts a project.

    Inputs are stored, not re-fetched
    Replay uses recorded provider responses, so no paid call is repeated and the answer cannot drift.
    Policy versions are pinned
    Scorecard, workflow, pricing script and guardrail pack — each resolved to what was live then.
    The rule path is preserved
    Which rules fired, on which values, against which thresholds — plus any human decision taken.

    Entity history

    What this loan, application or client looked like over time. Nothing is silently overwritten — a rollback preserves the original allocation.

    Activity log

    Who did what, across which records. Logins, views, approvals, edits, overrides and permission changes.

    Exception log

    Where the system itself is failing, with grouping, retry and escalation. A failed statutory notice is an incident, not a log line.

    Tamper-evident, not merely append-only

    "Immutable" is easy to claim and rarely mechanised. Here the audit record is append-only by database enforcement, hash-chained so that each entry commits to its predecessor, and anchored externally — so even a privileged insider rewriting history breaks the chain visibly rather than silently.

    The threat being designed against is an administrator with database access, not a distributed-consensus problem — which is why this is a hash chain with external anchoring rather than a blockchain.

    Regulatory packs, not forks

    The core stays market-agnostic. Guardrail rules, prescribed templates, rate conventions, bureau formats, supervisory returns and conduct rules live in a versioned, effective-dated pack — so a historical evaluation resolves against the pack in force at the time, and adding a market never destabilises an existing one.

    Concretely: Nepal's NRB and SEBON packs — the enacted Margin Trading Directive 2082, with a 5× net-worth cap, a 10%-of-facility single-client limit and a 4.5× funding-leverage cap — the UK FCA corridor pack, and, where a Gulf corridor or an Islamic product is added, a distinct AAOIFI Shariah pack that a Malaysia-approved structure could not satisfy.

    Shariah compliance is a ledger property

    Under a compliant sale-based contract the customer owes a fixed sale price from inception — profit is recognised over time, but nothing accrues. That reaches the ledger, so it is modelled in the kernel as a contract type rather than as a skin over an interest engine.

    The sharpest consequence is late payment. Compensation for actual loss may be recognised as income; a punitive penalty may not — it must go to charity. So the platform carries a charity payable balance distinct from income, with its own disbursement, reconciliation and reporting. That is a first-class ledger concept, not a memo field, and it invalidates any design that treats late fees as ordinary revenue. Rebate on early settlement is likewise a real operation, not a discount.

    Applicable rates are jurisdiction-pack configuration, never hard-coded — and the permitted contract set itself differs by market, so a structure accepted in Malaysia may be disfavoured in the Gulf. Product structures and market-specific rules require confirmation by a qualified adviser and local counsel.

    What the lending domain needs from EDX — and the one place it must diverge

    Lending is the Financial Services Domain in EDX's Platform → Domain → Product → Solution layering. Most of what it needs, EDX already provides. One default it must not inherit.

    Consumed from EDX — not built

    • —Identity and access: federation, groups, org hierarchy, policy, RBAC and SSO
    • —Reference data: country, currency and industry code sets with mapping and audit history
    • —Document management: storage, authenticity validation, expiry, versioning, archival
    • —Notifications across email, SMS and push, with regional rule support
    • —Process and task management — the manual-review work-queue primitive
    • —Enterprise integration, including the AI-assisted adapter generation path
    • —Business metadata and runtime/session services
    • —The metadata-driven UI runtime — one codebase to web, iOS and Android
    • —The AI and predictive framework: model registry, serving, retraining and MLOps

    Each of these was originally specified as a lending module. Consuming them instead is what turns a platform build into a domain build.

    The one divergence — please design for it

    EDX recommends one database per microservice vertical, with saga between them. The credit kernel cannot follow that default.

    Balances, credit operations, payment schedules and allocation must commit in a single ACID transaction. Eventual consistency between a payment and the balance it settles is the precise mechanism by which lending platforms develop subtle, expensive, late-discovered defects.

    The ask is narrow: allow the credit vertical to be sized to contain the whole ledger. Saga stays — but at the external edges, where it belongs: payment rails, the remittance leg, core-banking posting, commodity trades.

    Dependencies and open reconciliation

    These are live questions, not settled positions. They were logged when EDX was captured, and they belong to both teams jointly.

    Technology gravity already favours this

    All four estates already standardise on the same container orchestration, event streaming, relational storage, caching, object storage and observability stack the kernel needs. Compatibility is a starting condition, not something to engineer — which is why the credit kernel is the only genuinely new thing in the picture.

    The build decision

    EDX or standalone — and where saga actually belongs

    Both options are genuinely viable. The trade is platform reuse against independence — and one common assumption needs clearing up before the lists make sense.

    First, a correction worth making

    Saga is orthogonal to this choice. It is right at the external edges in both options, and wrong inside the ledger in both.

    Payment rails, the remittance leg, core-banking posting and commodity trades are genuinely distributed and need compensating transactions. Balances, credit operations, schedules and allocation are not — they must commit together or not at all.

    So a standalone build that puts saga between a payment and the balance it settles inherits exactly the same defect class, with none of the platform reuse to show for it. "Standalone with saga" is not the safer option — it is the weakest cell in the matrix.

    And keeping the ledger whole is not a violation of EDX. A vertical is a group of related services sharing one database, so a credit vertical sized to contain the ledger is that pattern applied correctly. The ask is narrow.

    Lending as a domain on EDX

    In favour

    • +Nine platform services consumed rather than built — the difference between a domain build and a platform build
    • +Metadata-driven UI in production: one codebase to web, iOS and Android, with fields and language shipping as configuration
    • +Integration adapters generated from the domain model and an interface spec, cutting connector effort substantially
    • +Model serving, registry, retraining and per-client tuning already exist — no MLOps stack to build for scorecards
    • +The solution layer is exactly the per-client, configuration-only release model each estate needs
    • +All four estates already run the same idioms, so integration is native rather than four bespoke seams
    • +No second platform to secure, patch, audit and staff

    Against

    • −The one-database-per-vertical default must be explicitly exempted for the kernel — a decision, not an assumption
    • −The process runtime is still under development, a real schedule dependency for a workflow-heavy domain
    • −The visual designer is not yet available; today low-code means builders and JSON, which is narrower than the label
    • −Metadata UI has limits: new widget types need a client update, and complex conditional logic is not configurable
    • −Release cadence couples to the platform's, and a platform regression becomes a lending incident
    • −Reverses documented lending decisions, so the architecture set needs re-baselining

    Standalone lending platform

    In favour

    • +Full control of the ledger boundary, the architecture and the release cadence — no shared roadmap to negotiate
    • +No dependency on the unfinished process runtime
    • +The cleanest story where lending must be governed as a self-contained system
    • +The air-gapped topology sits entirely within one team's control
    • +Sellable to a third-party lender without requiring them to adopt EDX underneath

    Against

    • −Every platform service becomes scope to build, operate and staff — identity, documents, notifications, work queue, reference data, integration, audit substrate, ML serving, reporting
    • −Channels built from scratch: separate web, iOS and Android codebases instead of one metadata-driven runtime
    • −Bespoke adapters per estate instead of native routes
    • −Duplicates capability the institution already governs — permanently. The cost is the operating surface, not just the build

    Recommendation

    Build on EDX for Swift's own estates, and package the same kernel to stand alone for institutions that will not take a platform underneath.

    Because the kernel is sold to any financial institution, this is not an either-or. Both shapes have to be first-class, and the architecture already assumes it — the same artifacts run from a single node to an air-gapped estate. On EDX the reuse is large and concrete and the estates already share its idioms; standalone, the platform services ship inside the deployment instead of being consumed from one.

    Two things make that affordable rather than a double build: the lending domain is identical either way, and the ledger boundary is the same in both. Settle the kernel exemption in writing before work starts, and actively manage the process-runtime dependency — get a date, and plan the workflow split so it does not wait on one.

    Where the discipline is needed

    Supporting both shapes only stays cheap if nothing platform-specific leaks into the domain. The moment a lending module calls an EDX service directly rather than through an interface, the standalone build becomes a fork — and the second deployment starts paying for the first.

    Deployment

    Standalone, or as a domain on EDX

    The lending capability is identical either way. What differs is how much surrounding platform the institution already owns.

    As a lending domain on EDX

    EDX is Swift's enterprise platform, organised in layers: a foundation providing the runtime and shared services, business domains built on top, products built on domains, and per-client solutions built on products. Swift Lending OS is the financial-services domain in that model.

    An institution already operating an enterprise platform should not acquire a second one alongside it. Identity, documents, notifications, task management and integration already exist and are already governed; the lending domain contributes what they do not have — the credit kernel and the lending modules.

    • —Ten EDX platform services consumed rather than built
    • —One codebase to web and mobile via the EDX UI runtime
    • —Per-client deployment through the EDX solution layer
    • —Native integration where host systems share the platform's idioms

    Standalone

    The platform ships its own identity, document, notification, task, integration and reporting services. Nothing external is required, and the same lending domain sits on top of them unchanged.

    This is the shape for institutions without an enterprise platform underneath, or where lending must be operated and governed as a self-contained system.

    The trade is real and worth stating. Every service in the middle band becomes scope the lending programme builds, operates and staffs. The lending capability is identical; the surrounding cost is not.

    • —Self-contained: no external platform dependency
    • —Identical lending capability and identical kernel guarantees
    • —Runs in every topology, including fully air-gapped
    • —Fully independent release cadence

    In both: the credit kernel keeps one transactional boundary, and saga appears only at external edges.

    The platform in practice

    Building the OneSphere pilot on EDX and Swift Lending OS

    A real lender, a real 43-page requirements document, and a concrete plan for standing up a licensed digital-lending pilot in Malaysia — on two platforms Swift already owns.

    The programme

    OneSphere intends to serve migrant workers in Malaysia — a segment traditional credit files describe poorly, but whose remittance behaviour is a genuine cash-flow signal.

    Two pilot products: Remit-Now-Pay-Later, credit extended to a sender at send-time and repaid later, and a short-tenure salary-linked loan. Corridor lending is the same mechanic wherever it runs — only the corridor, the partner and the regulatory pack change.

    What each layer supplies

    EDX is Swift's enterprise platform — a low-code development framework plus a full application runtime. Swift Lending OS is the lending domain built on it. Almost nothing in the pilot is new infrastructure; the build concentrates on credit truth and the Malaysian regulatory pack.

    EDX supplies the platform
    Ten reusable services, the metadata-driven UI runtime, the integration framework, the ML stack, and the whole runtime and CI/CD foundation. None of it is a lending build.
    Swift Lending OS supplies credit truth
    The ledger, credit operations, decisioning policy, servicing and collections. This is the part EDX does not provide, and the part where correctness cannot be compromised.
    OneSphere is a Solution
    In EDX's layering — Platform, Domain, Product, Solution — the pilot is a Solution: client-specific configuration only, with no Domain or Product logic written at that layer.
    Language is configuration
    A borrower journey in Nepali, Bengali or Bahasa Malaysia is presentation metadata and translation content — published centrally, picked up without an app-store release.

    The approval sequence shapes the build

    Verified against the regulator's own FAQ, checklist and guidelines: online moneylending approval is an endorsement on an existing licence, and what the regulator asks for is a documentation pack plus a system security certificate — not a live demonstration. A search of all three official documents found no published demo, inspection or system audit as an approval step.

    1. 01

      Conventional licence issued

      Critical path. Only a holder with more than 90 days of remaining validity may apply for the endorsement.

    2. 02

      Documented design + security certificate

      A practical plan of the site, application and customer journey, including the technology used.

    3. 03

      Conditional approval

      Assessed by an internal committee. Service standard is three to six months from a complete application being tabled.

    4. 04

      Build, register domain, full approval

      The public register names the specific URL or app tied to each approval, so an identifiable platform must exist.

    Why this favours building on EDX

    The first deliverable is a document describing the system and the technology behind it. That document is far stronger when the platform it describes already exists, already runs in production elsewhere, and already carries identity, encryption, secrets management, audit trails and observability as platform properties.

    The customer-journey and technology plan is therefore a first-class deliverable of the engagement — it is, quite literally, the application.

    The sequencing constraint

    OneSphere holds a conditional approval letter dated June 2026 — not an issued licence. The conventional licence must issue first, and only then can the online endorsement be applied for. A realistic path to full online approval is well over a year.

    That is an argument for a smaller, compliance-first first release — one that fails cheap if the licence answer is unfavourable.

    Four phases, smallest risky thing first

    PHASE 0

    Fit spike

    Prove the ledger pattern under EDX's data model, prove the decisioning path under real connector fan-out, and confirm which shared services are production-grade for this workload. Stood up as a single-node topology. The point is to convert the largest architectural assumption into a measured result before anything is frozen.

    PHASE 1

    Licence-readiness release

    The compliance-critical spine, plus the documentation the application requires: onboarding with KYC and consent, agreement generation with the attestation step designed in, the credit kernel and servicing ledger under the Malaysian guardrail pack, the audit trio, and access control. Commercially rich modules are deliberately deferred. Deliverables include the customer-journey and technology plan, the security certificate evidence, and a registered .my domain with a named application.

    PHASE 2

    Pilot operation

    Remit-Now-Pay-Later live on one corridor. Remittance regularity as the credit signal, scored on the EDX predictive framework with reason codes and model versioning. Repayment by direct debit through the payments module, collections with contact caps and quiet hours enforced by the platform, and portfolio reporting from the operational record.

    PHASE 3

    Second corridor

    A new corridor as a new Solution — configuration and a regulatory pack over the same Domain, not another build. This is the phase that turns a project into a product, and it is the reason the first build is worth doing on a platform at all.

    Three things this deployment must get right

    Running a lending domain on a general-purpose enterprise platform requires three decisions that lending, specifically, cannot get wrong.

    1. 01

      The kernel keeps one transactional boundary

      EDX recommends one database per microservice vertical with saga between them. That is right for most domains and wrong for a loan ledger. The credit vertical is sized to contain the whole ledger; saga appears only at external edges — payment rails, the remittance leg, core-banking posting.

    2. 02

      Lending brings its own process semantics

      EDX's Process Runtime is Camunda-based and still under development. The lending domain uses the underlying workflow engine directly, with the business-process and decision-workflow split it needs, rather than waiting on a generic abstraction. Plan for that dependency explicitly.

    3. 03

      Statutory limits sit outside the scripts

      The rate ceiling, the compounding prohibition and the fee prohibition belong in the guardrail layer, where a pricing-script edit cannot breach them. Under this statute a mistake is not a pricing error — sums paid are set off against the principal.

    What primary-source research changed

    Verified against the statute and the regulator's own published documents — findings the requirements document did not contain, each one changing the product before it was built.

    Finding 01

    The target market is at risk

    Published guidance advises that online loans be extended only to citizens and permanent residents — directly adverse to a migrant-worker product. Advisory rather than statutory, but approval runs through a discretionary committee. This is the first question to put in writing.

    Finding 02

    There is no separate online licence

    It is an endorsement on an existing moneylending licence, applied for physically, with no additional fee and no separate validity. Programme terminology that treats it as a distinct licence class misstates both the sequence and the eligibility test.

    Finding 03

    Agreements need human attestation

    The statute requires a qualified person to explain the terms and certify the borrower appears to understand them. An unattested agreement is void — the hardest constraint on a fully remote journey, and the requirements document does not address it anywhere.

    And one that decides a product

    The governing statute caps unsecured interest, prohibits compounding, and makes any additional fee illegal — with sums paid set off against principal. On a one-month tenure that leaves roughly 1.5% of principal as total revenue, with no fee available to bridge it.

    The short-tenure salary-linked product is very unlikely to be viable as specified. The remittance-linked product, at larger tickets and longer tenures, is materially stronger. That is a commercial finding worth more to a lender than the software that would have implemented it.

    If an Islamic variant is wanted, decide it before design

    Remit-Now-Pay-Later is genuinely hard to structure as Shariah-compliant, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than assuming it will work. The product hands a customer cash to remit, and cash cannot be lent at a profit. A commodity-trade structure works and is accepted in the market, but it is operationally heavy for a small, fast, low-margin financing. An interest-free loan paired with a genuine agency fee for the remittance service is the cleanest and least system-heavy route — but only if the fee reflects real service cost rather than profit on the loan in disguise, and that is a question only a Shariah committee can settle.

    The requirements document also treats late payment as ordinary revenue, which no compliant structure permits. Both points belong in a committee conversation that starts before product design, not after.

    Since then, primary-source research settled three more — and each one reached the guardrails

    Finding 04 · Margin

    Reconciled to the enacted directive

    Nepal's Margin Trading Directive 2082 (in force February 2026) superseded the secondary figures the plan carried — broker capacity is 5× net worth, not 2×; a single client is capped at 10% of the total facility, not 20% of net worth — and added a 4.5× funding-leverage limit and a 20% maintenance margin. Each reached the guardrails as a rule, not a note.

    Finding 05 · Corridors

    A remittance licence never lends

    Across five markets a remittance or exchange licence confers no lending permission on its own — a separately-licensed lender must originate. One corridor partner already runs exactly this, lending to foreign workers through its own licensed finance entity over the Gulf wage-protection rail. So the platform powers the licensed lender; the remittance operator distributes.

    Finding 06 · Islamic

    Sharia products don't cross borders

    A structure accepted in one market is not automatically accepted in another — commodity-Tawarruq is the backbone of Malaysian personal finance yet is disfavoured under the Gulf's AAOIFI standard. A product cannot simply be ported; each market gets its own Shariah pack, versioned and effective-dated like every other regulatory pack.

    Open before scope freeze

    Stated plainly, because each one changes the build and none of them is ours to answer alone.

    • Will the regulator approve online lending to documented work-permit holders?Nothing else matters if the answer is no. It should be put in writing first.
    • Does the conditional-approval tier still operate in 2026?It determines whether the platform is built before or after the application — the single largest planning variable.
    • What satisfies the system security certificate?A digital certificate from a licensed authority and a security assurance report are different procurements.
    • How is attestation performed in an online journey?Whatever mechanism is permitted shapes the entire customer flow, so it must be settled before UX design.

    One caveat recorded honestly: the regulator's 27-page guidelines document is an image-only scan and has been read only in part. Its full conduct, security, record-keeping and disclosure requirements remain the most important outstanding input to this plan.
